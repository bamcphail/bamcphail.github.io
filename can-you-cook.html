Can You Cook?

Product management isn't about your specific skills — it's about whether or not you can deliver.
August 2026  |  By Bryce McPhail  |  ← All blogs

I've been a product manager for a little over a decade now, and building products for longer than that. In that time I've watched the PM job description get rewritten dozens of times. Product managers were glorified project managers. Then we became product owners. Then it was SQL and technical skills, and the question became whether PMs should just become engineers. Then it was sales — could you actually sit across from a client and close. Now it's AI adoption and how well you can break down a user story. Every one of these eras convinced itself it had finally found the real answer.

The reality is that all of it matters. You do need to manage projects. You do need to speak with clients. You do need to uncover signal in a casual conversation or read it off the market itself. You do need to build. You do need to use AI tools in new and valuable ways. But I believe we've lost the plot. When I look at a product manager, I don't need a scorecard of their hard skills or their user stories or their AI fluency. I need to know one thing: can you cook. Can you throw down. Can you make it happen.

Can you go from a side comment in a casual conversation with a client to a full prototype they're willing to pay for, a deal that just needs a yes. Can you sit in seventeen different meetings, find the common thread, take a step back, run the OODA loop, and pull an entire team together so everyone is rowing in the same direction, creating organization out of chaos. Can you be the one who gets thrown to the wolves, the one people look to when things get tough because they already know you'll find an answer, find a way, make something from nothing. That's what I care about. That's how I measure PMs. Not someone who measures AI capabilities well or writes clean user stories. Someone who can step into the fire and come out alive.

And the best ones don't just step into the fire. They chase it. They're firemen. Rita, a teammate of mine, told me recently: "I've heard you're dangerous." In my mind, that's one of the best compliments a PM can get. It means your reputation precedes you, that people who haven't even worked with you yet already see you as someone who makes things happen and gets the job done.

My favorite show is The Bear. I love it for several reasons, but mostly because I think it's the perfect metaphor for startups, maybe not even a metaphor if you zoom out far enough. It follows a family-owned sandwich shop in Chicago, a failing business tangled up in family theater and deep character work. The show's main character, Carmy, is a world-class chef trying to resuscitate the family restaurant after the death of a family member, and to convince the people working there not just that they can be better than they are, but that they have to be, and that he can teach them how. The individual units, put together, become something far greater than the sum of their parts.

The Bear is quite literally about cooking. And I think it's the perfect metaphor for what I mean when I say the best PMs know how to cook. They need to be able to create. They need to find a morsel of signal — what actually matters to their market — and turn it into something. They need to look at a pile of ingredients and see the whole: a meal that isn't just edible, but desirable. They need to be able to corral a team, get them invested in one mission, and level them up in the effort of that mission.

The best PMs are not data scientists. They are not engineers. They are not project managers. They are not MBAs. They are not customer support, or marketing, or management. The best PMs are cooks. They understand the outcome deeply. They understand what each ingredient is, what it means, how it works, what it tastes like. They build a kitchen culture that gets everyone performing at the highest level, pulling the rope in the same direction. And more than anything, they cook. They get people out of their own way. They ask for more responsibility. They want the ball in the biggest moments. They believe in themselves, but they're humble enough to put their teammates first. They understand the entire act of the role is service. And then they go to work.

My favorite business quote is from Josh Wolfe of Lux Capital: chips on shoulders put chips in pockets. It's actually one of his investment theses. I first heard it in college, and I've carried it with me ever since.

If you know my story, you know I don't come from a long line of white-collar careers, or the kind of people who can get you a job by picking up the phone. I don't have a university name attached to mine that opens many doors. I've been denied more times than I can count, and I've had to force my way through every door instead of having one opened for me. That's what the great PMs do. They find a way. They own the outcome. They create something from nothing — signal from noise — and they find a way to win.

I think this matters as we figure out what a PM even is anymore, and what it's becoming. What's the actual difference between go-to-market, PM, engineering, and design at this point, with AI collapsing the distance between all of them? What's going to happen to any of us? At the end of the day, the question I keep coming back to is simpler than all of that: can you deliver outcomes. Can you deliver ARR that wasn't there before. Can you cook. As Carmy says in The Bear: can you hear the noise?

There's a scene where Carmy pulls one of his cooks, Sydney, off the line and asks her that same thing. He can see she's already great — she just can't see it in herself yet. He has her step back, close her eyes, and listen. When she locks in, she realizes she already knows the answer. She goes back to the line and nails it. That's the whole job. The best PMs are the ones who can hear the noise without anyone pulling them off the line to point it out.

My favorite scene in The Bear is a seven-minute monologue where Carmy is at an AA meeting, because he grew up in a house with alcoholism in the family. In it, he walks through his story — why he is the way he is, why he went off to prove himself because no one would let him cook in his mom and dad's restaurant that he is now trying to save.

So he forced his way into the best restaurants in the world. He put in the work. He learned. He started from the bottom. He smelled like food. He didn't have friends. He missed birthday parties. He grinded, and he grinded, and he grinded. And underneath all of it, he just wanted to come home and cook with his brother, who now owned the restaurant. But his brother told him no, time and time again.

Carmy goes on to say: "And that flipped a switch in me. I was like: okay, f--- you, watch this." And he went to another level. He obsessed over every ingredient. He drew every plate. He stayed late to take notes. He reflected on every moment and every interaction in the kitchen. He read new cookbooks. He forced his way into the best restaurants in the world. He learned from everyone around him. And as a result, he became the best. He became the one who delivered. He became the one who ran toward the fire. He became a fireman.

Sure, he had the knife skills. He learned to plate, to sauté, to dice, to grill. But at the end of the day, what mattered to Carmy's customers — his users — was simple: when they sat down, were they going to get the best culinary experience of their lives. And the answer was yes.

So what makes a great PM, in my mind? It's not their hard skills. It's not their soft skills. It's not their technical skills. It's not their go-to-market skills. It's not their AI skills. It's their capability to take all of those skills and put them together — to use them when the time is right, to look at each one as a tool on their belt that they pull out when the situation demands it. It's whether they're the person other people look to when backs are against the wall and the fire is raging, and whether they deliver, time and time and time again. When those PMs prove that they can deliver, remind them that they can hear the noise, and let them cook. And to the PMs themselves, let it rip.
